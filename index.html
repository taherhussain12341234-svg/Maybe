Left Behind
(00) A true story · est. 1967

Left Behind. Never forgotten.

Six machines that stayed on the Moon and Mars, and the work they still do.

Apr 1967 · Chapter 1

The brave robot that went first

C Robots like me always go first. Brave, right?

Before any astronaut stepped on the Moon, NASA sent Surveyor 3 to check if the ground was safe. It landed in Oceanus Procellarum and dug trenches—confirming the lunar soil was firm enough for Apollo.

Surveyor 3 had no rocket engine to fly home, so it stayed behind. Two years later (Nov 1969) Apollo 12’s Pete Conrad & Alan Bean walked to it, cut off its TV camera and scoop arm, and brought those parts back to Earth to study how space affects materials.

Why it stayed: No lift-off engine – a return trip would need too much fuel.
How it helps: The returned parts taught engineers about metal aging on the Moon. (Surveyor 3’s camera is now in a museum.)
July 1969 · Chapter 2

The mirror that was left to answer

C It sends light straight back, like a space boomerang!

At Tranquility Base, Armstrong & Aldrin left behind a panel of corner-cube mirrors (they then lifted off, leaving the rest of Eagle). Apollo 14 and 15 added more panels. These retroreflectors require no power – they just sit still on the Moon.

Scientists on Earth fire lasers at these panels and time the beam’s round-trip. This laser ranging experiment has run for decades. NASA reports it shows the Moon receding from Earth at about 3.8 cm per year.

Why it stayed: A mirror only works if it stays on the Moon.
How it helps: Lasers still bounce off these panels, enabling precise measurements of lunar distance.

July 1971 · Chapter 3

The car that stayed on the Moon

C Vroom! The first Moon car had no roof and no doors.

Apollo 15 carried the first Lunar Roving Vehicle (LRV), a battery-powered “moon buggy” for driving farther than walking range. Commander David Scott and Jim Irwin drove it around Hadley Rille, traversing hills and rocks up to ~28 km (17.3 mi) total.

When their mission was done, each kilogram mattered for the ride home. The astronauts parked the empty buggy on the plain, because the lander needed the weight capacity for ~77 kg of moon rocks instead. (They did record how it performed: today its design still guides modern rover missions.)

Why it stayed: Moon rocks (77 kg) mattered more – no spare fuel or space to fly the car back.
How it helps: It proved vehicles work on the Moon. Engineers still study its design when planning new Moon rovers.

Feb 2021 – Jan 2024 · Chapter 4

The helicopter that flew on Mars

C A helicopter on Mars? Zoom zoom!

In Feb 2021, NASA’s Perseverance rover landed at Jezero Crater on Mars, carrying *Ingenuity*, a small solar-powered helicopter. Ingenuity’s first flight (19 Apr 2021) made history as “the first aircraft to make a powered, controlled flight on another planet”.

Mars’ atmosphere is extremely thin (about 1% of Earth’s pressure), so to fly its two 1.2 m rotors had to spin very fast against very little air. Originally planned for just 5 flights, Ingenuity far outperformed expectations – it completed 72 flights over nearly three years. On Jan 18 2024, its final flight ended with a hard landing that broke a rotor blade. Now broken, Ingenuity is parked on Mars for the foreseeable future.

Why it stayed: It’s a test craft with a broken rotor blade, left on Mars (no humans or rockets there yet).
How it helps: Its success proved we can fly on Mars. Data from its flights is guiding new Mars aircraft designs.
2022–2023 · Chapter 5

The tubes that wait for a ride

C Ten tubes, all tucked in and waiting.

Perseverance not only drives and flies cameras – it also drills rock cores. It has filled dozens of metal sample tubes with Mars rock and dirt. In Jan 2023 the rover deposited ten sealed tube samples at a site called “Three Forks” as a backup stash on the Martian ground. The plan was for a future mission (NASA/ESA Mars Sample Return) to pick them up and bring them to Earth.

However, recent budget reviews have effectively canceled (or indefinitely delayed) the Mars Sample Return mission. For now, those sample tubes remain on Mars. Perseverance continues to study their contents with onboard instruments, and engineers hope one day another mission will retrieve them.

Why they stayed: No funded mission to bring them back – return rockets are extremely costly (NASA paused MSR in 2024).
How it helps: The tubes are sealed so samples stay safe. Perseverance’s own lab tools are analyzing them now. We’ll be ready if a future mission ever rescues these “astronaut-delayed” samples.

(09) Good to know.

Good to know.

Why not bring them home?

Every kilogram launched needs fuel and rockets. Return trips were designed for astronauts and rocks, not for old machines.

Is anything still talking to Earth?

Only Perseverance (on Mars) still sends data. Surveyor 3, the buggy and Ingenuity are silent relics. The Apollo mirrors require no power – lasers ping them.

Will the Mars tubes come home?

Not yet. The 2026 budget cut means no funded Mars Sample Return. Plans may change, so stay tuned to NASA news.

Can we still see them?

Yes! NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed Apollo landing sites from orbit. (No orbiters around Mars can image Perseverance closely.)

The ending

Still there. Still teaching.

A robot on a crater rim, a mirror panel on a lunar plain, a car parked in a valley, a helicopter with a bent blade on Mars, and ten sealed tubes on the red ground. None could come home, and none are finished.

Laser beams still bounce off the Moon’s mirrors. Engineers still learn from the rover and the helicopter. The tubes wait for the next explorers. These machines helped take our first steps on other worlds – and they keep inspiring us to go further.

Next time you see the Moon, remember: some of our things are still up there, keeping watch.
